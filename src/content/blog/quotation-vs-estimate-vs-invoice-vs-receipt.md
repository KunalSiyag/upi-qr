Ask a first-time business owner what they send a client, and the answer is usually "an invoice". Ask again and it turns out they mean a price list on WhatsApp, or a screenshot of an estimate, or — in the best case — an actual invoice followed by nothing at all.

Each stage of a client relationship has a correct document. Using the right one at the right time makes you look professional, keeps GST records clean, and dramatically improves how fast money moves. This guide separates the four documents once and for all.

## The 30-second answer

| Document | Sent | Legally binding? | Demands payment? | Proves payment? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Quotation** | Before agreement | Offer (binds if accepted as-is) | No | No |
| **Estimate** | Before agreement | No | No | No |
| **Invoice** | After delivery/agreement | Yes — triggers payment obligation | Yes | No |
| **Receipt** | After payment | Acknowledgement | No | **Yes** |

The flow is always the same shape:

**Quote → (client agrees) → deliver → Invoice → (money arrives) → Receipt**

## Quotation: your formal offer

A quotation is a fixed-price offer for defined scope. Its power comes from specificity: exact items, quantities, rates, taxes, and a validity date. When a client signs or replies "approved", a well-drafted quotation can function as a simple contract.

What separates a professional quotation from a price text message:

- A unique number (**QTN-0001**) so you can reference it in later documents
- Itemised lines instead of one lump figure
- Explicit tax treatment ("GST @18% extra" or "inclusive of all taxes")
- A **validity date** — fifteen to thirty days is standard; material-heavy quotes often run shorter
- Terms: advance percentage, delivery timeline, what is *not* included

Validity dates protect you from raw-material swings and from clients returning nine months later expecting old prices. ## Estimate: the honest sibling

An estimate looks like a quotation but says *"approximate"*. Use it when scope is genuinely uncertain — repairs, bespoke work, projects priced per discovery. Two rules keep estimates healthy:

1. State the assumption behind every variable line ("painting ₹12/sq ft assuming standard putty work").
2. Communicate early when reality drifts past the estimate. An estimate that silently becomes 40% higher on the final bill is how disputes are born.

Estimates do not need numbering discipline, though reusing your quote numbering costs nothing.

## Invoice: the document that creates the debt

The invoice is where things become official. It converts delivered value into a payable obligation, and in India a **tax invoice** under GST has mandatory contents:

- Your name, address, and **GSTIN**
- A **consecutive serial number** (unique for the financial year, max 16 characters)
- Date of issue
- Recipient details (and their GSTIN if registered)
- Description, quantity, and value of goods/services
- Tax amount charged, with rate
- Place of supply, and signature of the supplier or authorised representative

Numbering discipline matters more than most realise: INV-2026-001 → INV-2026-002 with no gaps is the pattern auditors like. Resetting numbers mid-year or skipping them invites questions.

For intra-state supplies you show CGST + SGST split; inter-state shows IGST. If you are not GST-registered, you issue a regular **bill of supply** instead and cannot charge GST — worth confirming before printing tax columns.

## Receipt: proof that closes the loop

The receipt is the only document that proves money actually moved. It records: amount received, date, mode (cash/UPI/bank), reference number, and against which invoice.

Three receipts cover most real life:

- **Payment receipt** — general business use; acknowledge advances, part-payments, and settlements with a [PAID-stamped receipt](/receipt-generator/)
- **Rent receipt** — the HRA-specific format with revenue stamp box; see [rent receipt format for HRA exemption](/blog/rent-receipt-format-hra-exemption-india/)
- **Acknowledgement slip** — for deliveries and document handovers

Issue receipts within minutes of payment, not month-end. A receipt sent instantly signals a business that has its act together — and gives the customer zero window to dispute what was paid.

## Common mixing mistakes

1. **Sending a quotation after work started.** By then it should be an invoice; sending a "quote" post-delivery reads as improvisation.
2. **Marking estimates as quotations.** Clients treat quoted prices as fixed promises. If scope is fuzzy, say "estimate".
3. **No invoice at all — just UPI requests.** Payment apps' collect requests prove nothing about what was purchased. The invoice is your sales record for GST input-credit buyers.
4. **Invoice without receipt.** Without acknowledgement, "I never paid" becomes possible. Attach receipts to settlements always.
5. **Reusing invoice numbers across years without prefixes.** FY-based prefixes (INV-2627-…) prevent painful confusion during filing.

## A worked example

You run a design studio. A café owner asks for branding work.

1. You inspect the brief and send **QTN-0031**: logo, menu design, signage artwork — ₹45,000 + 18% GST, valid until 30 September, 50% advance.
2. She approves and pays ₹22,500 advance. You issue **receipt RCPT-0117** referencing QTN-0031.
3. Work completes in three weeks. You raise **INV-2026-058** for the full ₹53,100, noting advance adjusted, balance ₹30,600 due in seven days.
4. She pays via your embedded UPI QR; instant **receipt RCPT-0118** marks INV-2026-058 fully settled.

Four documents, four unambiguous moments, zero WhatsApp archaeology later.
